# Attribution

The textheat source, English lexicon, demo, and documentation are original to this project. There are no mandatory runtime dependencies or third-party lexicon datasets.

The expanded dictionary in [src/core/lexicon-expanded.ts](../src/core/lexicon-expanded.ts) is an original project-authored selection of English review, product/service, and everyday emotional vocabulary. Its unchanged 1,326 authored keys comprise 1,046 single words and 280 exact phrases. The 101-entry original baseline takes precedence over 35 overlapping keys, giving the original 1,392 combined entries, including 291 phrases.

The additional original vocabulary in [src/core/lexicon-domains.ts](../src/core/lexicon-domains.ts) supplies **4,250 distinct new keys: 536 words and 3,714 phrases**. These cover everyday affect; purchasing, value, service and delivery; product construction; technology usability, reliability and privacy; travel, lodging, food and hospitality; education and workplace cooperation; accessibility, safety, fairness and ethics. This layer and the earlier expansion/baseline comprise the unchanged **5,642 pre-review entries: 1,637 words and 4,005 phrases**.

The original review addition in [src/core/lexicon-reviews.ts](../src/core/lexicon-reviews.ts) contributes **339 distinct new keys: 213 words and 126 short phrases**, individually selected and explicitly written rather than produced by Cartesian templates. It covers emotional states and reactions, praise and criticism, interpersonal conduct, technology reliability and functionality, product construction, and customer experience. Examples include “overjoyed” (+3.2, joy), “dysfunctional” (−2.6), “masterful” (+2.8), “silently drops my changes” (−2.7), “handles interruptions gracefully” (+2.3), and “felt genuinely listened to” (+2.2). Conservative spelling and inflection alternatives are enumerated, not inferred. Existing “malfunctioning” remains unchanged at −2; it is not duplicated. Bare plural observation heads such as “problems”, “issues”, “bugs”, “errors”, and “delays”, bare “support”, and observation frames such as “don't see …” are not new dictionary entries. Context-only observation handling is separate from this authored vocabulary.

The merged dictionary has **5,981 unique entries: 1,850 words and 4,131 phrases**, with a maximum phrase length of **12 words**. Reviews are merged first, followed by the existing domain, expansion and baseline layers in their original order, preserving every one of the 5,642 previous scores and emotion assignments. There are no duplicates within the review layer and no overlaps with the old merged dictionary. The frozen `lexiconStats` object in [src/core/lexicon.ts](../src/core/lexicon.ts) reports actual unique merged counts, not source-row totals or estimates of English coverage.

New words, inflections and spelling variants are explicitly enumerated. The older domain layer's phrase generation uses compact, locally authored compatibility lists, not arbitrary combinations of words: 20 review families each pair ten singular subjects with eight positive and eight negative evaluative predicates in the single frame “the SUBJECT was PREDICATE” (3,200 exact phrases). Five first-person affect groups each pair ten feelings with eight everyday occasions in “i felt FEELING OCCASION” (400 phrases). A further 114 domain phrases are individually authored, including qualified readings of ambiguous words and short idiomatic opinions. The new review layer does not generate any phrase combinations. All phrases become exact keys; no stemmer, runtime network access, model, or additional dependency is involved. No external sentiment dataset, dictionary export, fitted score table, copyrighted external lexicon, or third-party lexicon was used or copied.

Scores and emotion assignments are conservative, manually authored heuristics, not empirical measurements or calibrated probabilities. Existing domain review templates use shared modest scores (+2 or −2.2), not individually measured ratings; word and affect groups share manually chosen scores and, where appropriate, emotion labels. New review entries use explicitly authored scores and optional emotion labels. Strongly ambiguous standalone terms such as “fine”, “sound”, “cold”, “sick”, “cheap”, “charge”, and “patient” are omitted; some words still depend on context. For example, “flaky” is intended as a reliability criticism but can occur in a factual description of pastry, and “insecure” can describe either an emotional state or technology security. Qualified phrases express specific evaluative readings instead. Phrase length is automatically derived from the merged keys, and internal negation is included in the assigned meaning; preceding external negation remains subject to the existing scope rules. Matching preserves source UTF-16 offsets and accepts spaces/tabs, but does not bridge punctuation, hyphens, or newlines.

The lexical tests check all addition keys for normalization, tokenizer compatibility, duplicate keys, bounded finite scores and valid emotions; exercise every addition as a complete lexical span; fingerprint both the 1,392 legacy values and all 5,642 pre-review values; reject review overlaps with every previous layer; and cover representative domains, neutral homonyms, phrase boundaries, negation and source offsets. Every new phrase is tested against punctuation, newline and hyphen boundaries. These checks establish implementation consistency, **not measured sentiment accuracy**. Template coverage and individually authored review phrases are intentionally restricted to exact keys, not all tenses, paraphrases or grammatical contexts. Questions, quotations, hypothetical assertions and sarcasm can still match a phrase without expressing the writer's own opinion. Broader coverage does not supply word-sense disambiguation, sarcasm detection, a trained POS tagger, or a grammatical parser; emotion nouns and verbs added here still receive the existing approximate POS treatment. Any externally supplied calibration should be reevaluated after a vocabulary change.

Contextual emphasis rules are inspired by the general VADER approach (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014, “VADER: A Parsimonious Rule-based Model for Sentiment Analysis of Social Media Text”); no VADER source code, licensed lexicon, or fitted scores are copied. Native browser AI integration follows the browser Prompt API interface; model licensing, storage, and availability are managed by the browser vendor, not bundled by textheat.
